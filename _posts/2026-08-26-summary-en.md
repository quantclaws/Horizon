## [KellyBoost: Growth-Optimal Portfolio Construction Using Gradient-Boosted Trees](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23393) ⭐️ 8.0/10

KellyBoost introduces a single multi-output XGBoost model whose softmax output represents portfolio weights, trained by minimizing the negative log growth rate (−log(1+ wᵀy)) to directly learn Kelly‑optimal allocations conditioned on input features. By providing an exact, surrogate‑free loss for the Kelly criterion within gradient‑boosted trees, the method bridges machine learning and quantitative finance, offering a principled way to construct growth‑optimal portfolios that could improve long‑term wealth growth for investors and inspire further ML‑based finance research. The model’s softmax yields long‑only, fully‑invested weights on the simplex; the paper derives the gradient, analytic diagonal Hessian and full Hessian in closed form, verifies them via finite differences, and supplies a dependency‑free pure‑NumPy reference engine with XGBoost as an optional fast backend.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Aug 25, 04:00

**Background**: The Kelly criterion maximizes the expected logarithmic growth of wealth, which is equivalent to maximizing the long‑term geometric growth rate of a portfolio. Gradient‑boosted trees, exemplified by XGBoost, are powerful supervised learning models that optimize differentiable loss functions using gradient and Hessian information. Multi‑output XGBoost can predict a vector of outputs per instance, allowing a single model to allocate weights across multiple assets when its outputs are transformed via softmax.

## [The Measurement Revolution? Credible Measurement and Inference in the Age of AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23524) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The authors of arXiv:2608.23524v1 propose a three‑stage AI‑driven measurement pipeline—discovery, construct definition, and observation—and argue that credible inference requires validation anchored to explicit criteria rather than informal proxy assumptions. This framework clarifies how to use AI for economic measurement while addressing validation and inference challenges, potentially shaping empirical research practices if adopted by the economics community. The pipeline’s three stages each impose distinct demands on researchers, and validation is defined as anchoring measurement to explicit, observable criteria; the paper shows that even arbitrarily biased AI predictions can yield valid inference when supported by appropriate validation samples, though a random validation sample may not always be available.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Aug 25, 04:00

**Background**: AI enables economists to turn unstructured data such as text and images into structured variables at low cost, making previously infeasible measurements scalable. This shifts the bottleneck from finding any scalable measure to choosing among many plausible AI‑generated measures that may lead to different empirical conclusions. Credible inference therefore depends on validation that anchors these measures to explicit criteria rather than relying on informal assumptions about proxy reasonableness.

## [The Price of Isolation: Estimating the Ecosystem Cost of Symmetric Two-Sided A/B Testing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04432) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The authors show that, in an order-statistics model of engagement on two-sided platforms, the engagement cost of symmetric two-sided isolation does not necessarily disappear as the platform grows; instead, its asymptotic behavior depends on the upper tail of match quality. Using extreme-value theory, they derive tail-class loss laws: for light or bounded tails the cost vanishes with larger candidate pools, whereas for heavy tails it converges to a size-independent constant. The result provides experimenters with a quantitative way to anticipate and budget the isolation cost before launching creator‑side or cold‑start experiments, helping avoid unexpected engagement losses. It also clarifies when simply scaling up a platform will not mitigate experimental interference, influencing design choices for large marketplace and content platforms. Using an order-statistics model of viewer engagement, the authors apply extreme-value theory to show that the isolation cost vanishes for light or bounded match-quality tails but approaches a size-independent constant for heavy tails. They validate this dichotomy with two production experiments on a platform of millions of creators—an A/A sweep reveals a depth-graded cost, catalog ablation links the loss to per-viewer thinning, and a tail index from a small exploration pool predicts the full-catalog effect—and provide a preflight procedure to estimate the cost, size traffic accordingly, and suggest a fallback design when the cost exceeds a tolerance.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Aug 25, 04:00

**Background**: Symmetric two-sided isolation assigns matched fractions of creators and viewers to isolated treatment and control submarkets, eliminating cross-arm interference in marketplace experiments but also thinning each viewer’s candidate catalog. In an order-statistics model, a viewer’s engagement is assumed to depend on the highest match quality among the remaining candidates, making the cost of thinning dependent on the upper tail of the match-quality distribution. Extreme-value theory characterizes how the maximum of many samples behaves, distinguishing light/bounded tails (where the maximum grows slowly with sample size) from heavy tails (where the maximum stabilizes, leading to a non-vanishing isolation cost).

## [Does Rank Still Matter? Position Bias When AI Agents Shop on Our Behalf](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22697) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Researchers randomized the order of 100 hotel listings across 5,000 AI agent sessions and tested four large language models, finding that AI agents show weaker, non‑monotonic position bias—inspecting middle results least—and converge on the same undominated listing regardless of model. The results suggest that when AI agents handle search, the visual attributes shown on a results page outweigh raw ranking, which could shift e‑commerce design and SEO strategies toward richer item displays rather than mere position bidding. Position still predicts which listings are inspected, but the relationship is weak and non‑monotonic: the middle of a page has the lowest inspection probability, not the bottom. While some models let position affect the final choice, others do not, a split unrelated to provider or model capability, yet all models ultimately select the same undominated (Pareto‑optimal) listing.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Aug 25, 04:00

**Background**: Position bias is the tendency of users to examine and click higher‑ranked items more often, regardless of relevance, which distorts click‑based training of ranking models. AI agents can ingest an entire results page at once, potentially reducing reliance on sequential scanning. An undominated listing is one that is not worse than any other listing on all attributes, i.e., a Pareto‑optimal option that no other option dominates.
