## [What Would it Cost to End Extreme Poverty?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02013) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The study frames poverty minimization via direct cash transfers as a statistical learning problem, using household surveys from 34 countries representing 76% of the world's poor, and estimates that lowering the extreme poverty rate from 13% to 1% would require about $211 billion per year. The figure shows that ending extreme poverty is affordable, amounting to only 0.28% of global GDP, which is far less than the cost of a universal basic income and only four times the aggregate poverty gap reduction. The estimate is 4.0 times the reduction in the aggregate poverty gap and just 19% of the cost of a universal basic income; extrapolating the sample to the globe yields a cost of 0.28% of global GDP.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Sep 3, 04:00

**Background**: Extreme poverty is commonly defined as living on less than $2.15 per day (2017 PPP), and the poverty gap measures the average shortfall of the poor from that line. Direct cash transfer programs give money to households without conditions, aiming to raise consumption above the poverty line. The paper treats the selection of transfer amounts as a statistical learning problem under real‑world information constraints, using nationally representative household consumption surveys from 34 countries. These surveys cover about 76% of the world's poor, allowing the authors to extrapolate cost estimates globally.

## [Insights on Time-consistent Deep Hedging under Elicitable Dynamic Risk Measures.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02014) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The paper extends deep hedging to high-dimensional basket option hedging using time-consistent dynamic risk measures, demonstrating feasibility and analyzing how scoring function choice affects training versus static risk measure baselines. By linking conditional elicitability of spectral risk measures to deep hedging, the work offers a novel methodological bridge between machine learning and quantitative finance, potentially improving hedging of complex, multi-asset derivatives. The authors rely on the conditional elicitability of spectral risk measures to formulate the optimization objective, examine how different scoring functions influence agent training, and benchmark the resulting time-consistent strategies against deep hedging based on static risk measures that lead to pre‑commitment.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Sep 3, 04:00

**Background**: Deep hedging uses reinforcement learning to derive hedging strategies that minimize a risk measure of the portfolio profit and loss, traditionally applied with static risk measures leading to pre‑commitment. Time‑consistent dynamic risk measures require that decisions made today remain optimal in the future, which can be represented via conditionally elicitable spectral risk measures and their associated scoring functions. Basket options, whose payoff depends on multiple underlying assets, constitute a high‑dimensional hedging problem where simple low‑dimensional approaches often fail.

## [LLM Agents Show Slower Convergence in Double‑Auction Markets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02580) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Researchers replicated seminal double‑auction economic experiments, substituting human traders with LLM agents from multiple model families. They observed that markets with LLM agents converged to equilibrium more slowly or failed to converge, resulting in less efficient resource allocations, and they publicly released their evaluation framework. The result highlights a key limitation of current LLMs when acting as autonomous economic agents, suggesting that market mechanisms designed for humans may not function efficiently with AI participants. This insight is crucial for aligning AI behavior with economic efficiency and for designing safer multi‑agent systems. Analysis revealed substantial differences in trading behavior across LLM families and between buyer versus seller roles, and a lexical analysis of Chain‑of‑Thought traces showed that deciding to execute a trade correlates with a shift from strategic reasoning to urgency. The study employed a standard continuous double‑auction setup where agents submit bids and asks until a match occurs, allowing measurement of convergence speed and allocation efficiency.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Sep 3, 04:00

**Background**: In a double‑auction market, buyers submit bids and sellers submit asks; a trade occurs when a bid meets or exceeds an ask, and the mechanism is known to converge quickly to an equilibrium price when participants are human. Large language models are being used as autonomous agents in various economic tasks, yet their ability to follow the strategic reasoning required in such markets has not been empirically tested. The paper replicates classic double‑auction experiments with LLM agents to test whether these AI participants can achieve the same market efficiency and equilibrium convergence as humans.

## [Researchers develop ML measures to distinguish liquidity-supplying and demanding HFT.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00858) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Using proprietary Nasdaq data, researchers trained machine learning models to map high-frequency trading activity to public intraday variables, generating daily measures of liquidity-supplying and liquidity-demanding HFT for all U.S. stocks from 2010 to 2023. The new measures improve upon standard proxies by separating supply‑ and demand‑side HFT, enabling more accurate studies of market quality and generalizing across markets as shown with Euronext Paris data. Models were trained on proprietary Nasdaq data, validated with proprietary Euronext Paris data, and shown to subsume standard proxies while capturing time‑series variation they miss; supply‑side HFT correlates with lower spreads and more informed trading.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Sep 3, 04:00

**Background**: High-frequency trading (HFT) refers to algorithmic trading strategies that execute large numbers of orders in very short time frames, but public data do not label which trades are HFT. Standard proxies such as trade volume or turnover cannot differentiate between liquidity‑supplying (market‑making) and liquidity‑demanding (aggressive) HFT strategies, limiting research on market quality.
