## [The Privacy Subsidy: Kyle's λ under Noise-Perturbed Order-Flow Observation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15746) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The paper derives a linear Kyle equilibrium when a Bayesian market maker observes order flow perturbed by independent Gaussian privacy noise, showing that price impact and informed trading rescale by a single privacy factor and identifying a closed‑form privacy subsidy from liquidity providers to traders. The result provides a principled way to quantify the cost of privacy in shielded AMMs and DeFi designs, informing fee setting and welfare analysis for privacy‑preserving trading mechanisms. The price‑impact coefficient λ and the informed trader’s demand slope both scale by the same privacy factor, leaving their product invariant; the welfare decomposition yields an explicit per‑period transfer (privacy subsidy) from the LP pool to traders, analogous to the single‑period LVR formula.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · May 18, 04:00

**Background**: The Kyle model describes a market maker setting price as a linear function of total order flow when informed traders trade on private information. Loss‑versus‑rebalancing (LVR) measures the cost to AMM liquidity providers from stale prices exploited by arbitrageurs. Shielded AMMs add privacy noise to the observable order flow, altering what the pricing mechanism sees.

## [: From Model Design to Organizational Design: Complexity Redistribution in Generative AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22440) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The paper introduces the Generality-Accuracy-Simplicity (GAS) framework to analyze how large language models shift complexity from users to organizations, based on research by Sharique Hasan, Alexander Oettl, and Sampsa Samila released in June 2025. It reveals that competitive advantage in AI depends not on mere adoption but on mastering the redistributed complexity through abstraction layers, workflow alignment, and complementary expertise, reshaping organizational strategy. The GAS framework highlights an inherent trade‑off among generality, accuracy, and simplicity; while LLMs provide high generality and accuracy via simple interfaces, they move complexity to infrastructure, compliance, and specialized personnel, requiring firms to design abstraction layers and align workflows to retain advantage.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · May 18, 04:00

**Background**: Large language models (LLMs) are AI systems that can generate human‑like text across many tasks, often accessed through simple chat interfaces. Traditional theory, exemplified by Thorngate’s postulate of commensurate complexity, holds that a model cannot simultaneously be maximally general, accurate, and simple. The GAS framework adapts this idea to argue that LLMs appear to break the trade‑off for users but actually shift the unresolved complexity to organizational layers such as infrastructure, governance, and expert teams. Understanding this redistribution helps managers design effective AI integration strategies.
