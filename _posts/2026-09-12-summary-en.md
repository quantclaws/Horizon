## [EPA proposes to scrap public review for data center pollution permits.](https://capitalbnews.org/data-centers-permit-rules-epa/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is proposing to eliminate the public participation and comment requirements for New Source Review permits governing new or expanded data center pollution sources. This move would streamline permitting but reduce community oversight. Removing public review could lead to increased emissions from data centers without adequate scrutiny, impacting local air quality and public health, while benefiting developers seeking faster project approvals. The change reflects a broader trend of regulatory rollbacks that may shift the balance between economic development and environmental protection. The EPA’s New Source Review (NSR) program currently requires a preconstruction permit and public notice for any new major source or major modification, including data centers that emit pollutants such as NOx and PM2.5. The proposed rule would allow developers to begin construction of foundations and building shells before a final air permit is issued, while eliminating the mandatory public comment period.

hackernews · doener · Sep 11, 18:05 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49662672)

**Background**: The New Source Review (NSR) permitting program, established by the 1977 Clean Air Act Amendments, regulates new stationary sources and major modifications to existing sources to ensure air quality standards are met. Public participation is a core component of NSR, requiring notice and opportunity for comment before permits are finalized, as mandated by the Clean Air Act and detailed in 40 CFR 51.160. For data centers, NSR applies when they construct or expand facilities that increase emissions of pollutants such as nitrogen oxides or particulate matter.

## [Expected Shortfall Factor Model Captures Common Tail Loss Severity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10587) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The paper introduces an expected shortfall factor model (ESFM) that estimates common variation in the severity of lower‑tail losses by combining observed risk exposures with latent factors, using an orthogonalized two‑step procedure where first‑stage quantile estimation error has no first‑order effect on ES coefficients. Applied to a large equity panel, ESFM reveals factors that react sharply to market stress and generate high‑minus‑low portfolio returns of 8.0%–11.7% annually, with Fama‑French five‑factor alphas of 10.3%–15.0%. ESFM identifies a distinct priced dimension of downside risk—common loss severity—that is not spanned by traditional mean or quantile factors, offering a new tool for tail‑risk measurement and asset pricing. By showing that adding ESFM to standard factor sets raises the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio, the model suggests potential improvements in risk‑adjusted portfolio construction and risk management practices. The model is estimated via an orthogonalized two‑step procedure that eliminates first‑stage quantile estimation error from affecting ES coefficient estimates; the authors provide nonasymptotic error bounds, a finite‑sample Gaussian approximation, and a consistent information‑criterion based method for selecting the number of latent factors. Empirically, ESFM factors retain significant alphas after controlling for mean factors, quantile factors, and standard traded factors, and they improve the maximum Sharpe ratio when added to benchmark factor models.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Sep 11, 04:00

**Background**: Expected shortfall (ES) is a coherent risk measure that quantifies the average loss exceeding a value‑at‑risk threshold, thus capturing tail severity beyond the probability of loss. Factor models decompose asset returns into common factors and idiosyncratic components; mean factor models explain variation in average returns, while quantile factor models explain shifts in tail thresholds. ESFM extends this framework by modeling the common variation in the average magnitude of losses below those thresholds, filling a gap in modeling the severity of extreme losses.

## [Market-Informed Network Model Improves Forecasting of Financial Extremes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11575) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The paper introduces a time-dependent network Hüsler–Reiss model that uses market-informed adjacency matrices (binary and weighted, including the Joint Extremes Adjacency Matrix) to estimate joint extreme values in high-dimensional financial time series. Evaluated on one-minute S&P 100 stock returns, the JEAM specification yields 12.5‑14.9% better out‑of‑sample log scores for both lower and upper tails. Improving the forecast of financial extremes enhances risk management and regulatory reporting for investors and institutions. The work shows how incorporating market structure into extreme value models can yield substantial gains, offering a template for other high‑dimensional temporal domains. The model proposes binary and weighted specifications for market‑informed adjacency matrices, with JEAM combining individual extremeness and historical joint extreme movement patterns. In experiments, JEAM improves lower‑tail log scores by 12.5‑13.6% and upper‑tail scores by 11.4‑14.9% over baselines.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Sep 11, 04:00

**Background**: Extreme value theory studies the statistical behavior of rare, large events; the Hüsler–Reiss model is a max‑stable multivariate extreme value model derived from normalized Gaussian vectors, capturing complex extremal dependence via a variogram matrix and allowing closed‑form likelihoods. In high‑dimensional financial time series, extreme observations are sparse and locally extreme may not be extreme globally, motivating the use of network structures where adjacency matrices weight each observation's contribution. Market‑informed adjacency matrices embed information such as individual extremeness and past joint extreme movements to improve estimation.

## [Researchers propose an early asset‑pricing model for compute futures.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12156) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The paper introduces an early asset‑pricing framework for compute futures, linking spot and futures prices via expectations and risk premia in the non‑storable compute market. It provides a theoretical foundation for pricing a new class of tradable AI compute assets, which could improve risk management and investment decisions as AI‑related compute spending exceeds 1% of U.S. GDP. The model shows that direct no‑arbitrage links fail because compute cannot be stored, synthetic futures from term rental contracts act as upper bounds, and financialized futures prices equal expected spot prices minus a risk premium.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Sep 11, 04:00

**Background**: Compute (GPU‑hours) is a critical, capital‑intensive input for AI, with rental markets already exceeding 1% of U.S. GDP and growing rapidly. Unlike traditional storable commodities, compute cannot be inventoried, so standard no‑arbitrage pricing of forwards does not apply directly. Futures contracts for such non‑storable assets must rely on expectations of future spot prices adjusted for a risk premium, often approximated using synthetic positions from existing term rental agreements.
