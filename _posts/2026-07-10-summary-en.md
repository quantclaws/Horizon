## [PCA and Generative AI Combine for Benchmark‑Free Risk Modeling of Global Funds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07465) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The paper proposes using PCA on a fund's own returns to extract internal risk factors and adds a generative‑AI labeling layer to name those factors, creating a benchmark‑free risk model. It also introduces auxiliary scores (PRS, Bleed, timing gate) and tests on a 30‑stock global‑innovation proxy over one year. Traditional risk models rely on external benchmarks, which fail for funds without a suitable index; this method returns to an internal covariance view, offering interpretable, benchmark‑free factors that can improve risk measurement for niche or innovative portfolios. It could influence quantitative finance practice by providing a transparent alternative to Barra‑type or CAPM models. The approach uses PCA to rank factors by their actual contribution to portfolio risk, then applies a deterministic generative‑AI labeling rubric to name them without inventing structure. Additional signals include density‑based clustering with a mismatch ratio, a sign‑invariant PCA Risk Score (PRS), a Bleed score for slow capital loss, and a trailing‑return timing gate that routes signal‑price disagreements to human judgment.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Jul 9, 04:00

**Background**: Risk modeling traditionally measures a portfolio’s risk as its deviation from a benchmark index (e.g., market beta in CAPM or style/industry axes in Barra models). Principal component analysis (PCA) is a statistical technique that identifies orthogonal directions capturing the most variance in a dataset, often used to uncover latent factors in financial returns. Generative AI can produce human‑readable labels for abstract patterns, and when combined with PCA it helps make the otherwise opaque factors interpretable. The paper builds on these ideas to create a benchmark‑free risk framework that relies solely on a fund’s own return covariance.

## [tsbootstrap: Unified Library for Time Series Bootstrap and Conformal Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06690) ⭐️ 8.0/10

tsbootstrap introduces a typed API that combines block, residual, sieve, and wild bootstrap methods with adaptive conformal calibrators (EnbPI, ACI, NexCP, AgACI) for time series data. It provides distribution‑free uncertainty quantification and improves coverage and computational efficiency over i.i.d.‑based approaches. Many real‑world streams such as finance, sensor networks, and demand forecasts violate the exchangeability assumption of standard conformal prediction and bootstrap, leading to invalid intervals. tsbootstrap fills this gap by offering dependence‑aware resampling and conformal methods, giving practitioners reliable uncertainty estimates for dependent data. The library is released under MIT license (v0.6.1) and includes a compiled backend that runs several times faster than the arch baseline, while a streaming reduce limits extra memory to O(B) instead of O(Bn). Empirical results show the i.i.d. bootstrap severely undercovers under dependence, whereas sieve‑based bootstrap attains coverage closest to nominal for short‑memory linear processes.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Jul 9, 04:00

**Background**: Bootstrap methods estimate the sampling distribution of a statistic by resampling observed data; classical versions assume independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations. Conformal prediction builds prediction intervals that are valid under exchangeability, which also fails for dependent time series. When data exhibit temporal dependence, i.i.d.‑based bootstrap and conformal methods can produce miscalibrated intervals, motivating the need for block, residual, sieve, or wild bootstrap variants and adaptive conformal calibrators that respect dependence.

## [ContestTrade: Multi-Agent LLM Trading System with Internal Contest Mechanism](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00554) ⭐️ 8.0/10

ContestTrade introduces a multi-agent LLM trading framework that employs an internal 'Quantify-Predict-Allocate' contest mechanism. In a post-2024 A-share backtest, it outperforms baseline strategies in return and risk-adjusted performance. By scoring agent outputs only after market outcomes are observable and allocating resources based on predicted future utility, ContestTrade mitigates sensitivity to noisy, non‑stationary market data. This approach could improve the robustness of LLM‑driven trading systems and inspire similar contest‑based designs in other AI‑finance applications. The system splits into a Data Team that condenses market data into textual factors for LLM context windows and a Research Team that generates multipath trading decisions via tool‑augmented deep research. Agents are scored after outcomes are known, their future utility is predicted from past scores, and resources are allocated to those with positive predicted utility.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Jul 9, 04:00

**Background**: Large language model‑based agents have shown promise in processing unstructured financial information, but their decisions can be volatile when faced with noisy or shifting market conditions. Multi‑agent systems distribute tasks among specialized agents to improve robustness and scalability. Contest mechanisms, inspired by internal competitions in institutional investment teams, incentivize agents by rewarding those whose past performance predicts future utility.

Using unprecedented firm-level transaction data covering 20 million firms and 1 billion cross-border transactions, the authors constructed a directed network to map global production networks and identified three major product clusters. The study provides a large‑scale empirical foundation for understanding global supply chains, offering insights that can guide trade policy, investment decisions, and resilience planning. The analysis covered 20 million firms and 1 billion transactions, grouping products into textiles‑chemicals‑food, machinery‑metals, and a third cluster; European industrial nations and China were found to dominate critical intermediate products such as metals, common components and tools.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Jul 9, 04:00

**Background**: Global supply chains consist of the network of firms that produce, transform, and distribute goods across borders, often studied through input‑output tables or customs data. Firm‑level transaction data capture the actual buying and selling relationships between individual companies, enabling a more granular view of production linkages. Upstream (backward) linkages refer to a firm’s suppliers, while downstream (forward) linkages refer to its customers; both are used to assess how deeply a country is embedded in the production of a product. Industrial complexity measures the diversity and sophistication of a country’s export basket, reflecting its capacity to produce complex goods.

## [LLM summaries distort financial decisions.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29251) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The study shows that LLM‑based compression of financial filings and earnings‑call transcripts can produce fluent, factually plausible summaries that nevertheless change downstream investment decisions. This distortion arises from decontextualization and model dependency, where salient evidence is separated from its qualifiers or different compressors yield divergent views. Understanding how LLM compression affects decision‑making is crucial for deploying AI safely in high‑stakes finance, where even subtle shifts can lead to significant financial losses. The findings urge practitioners to evaluate compression methods not only for efficiency and factuality but also for their ability to preserve decision‑relevant context. The researchers identified two diagnostic patterns of fidelity loss: decontextualization, where key evidence is retained but detached from necessary caveats, and model dependency, where different LLMs produce varying compressions of the same source. To mitigate this, they propose Agentic Context Compression, which generates multiple candidate compressions and audits their disagreements against the original source.

rss · arXiv Quantitative Finance · Jul 9, 04:00

**Background**: Financial professionals often face overwhelming volumes of data from filings, earnings calls, and reports, necessitating concise summaries for timely decisions. Large language models are increasingly used to compress such texts, but ensuring that the compressed version preserves the original decision‑relevant context remains an open challenge. This paper introduces the notion of information fidelity to measure whether a compression alters the investment judgment derived from the source.
